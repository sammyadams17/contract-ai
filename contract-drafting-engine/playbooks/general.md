# General playbook

The rules every contract follows, whatever its type and whoever the client. A client's playbook
for one contract type starts with `@include` of this file and adds its own sections after it.

Each rule is a bullet under `## General`. Every bullet reaches every piece of the contract, so a
rule here must make sense wherever it lands; a rule for one clause belongs in that clause's
section of the client's playbook instead.

## General

- Every term the term sheet sets is written into the contract, once, in the place the base
  document keeps that subject. A term the base has no place for is written where a lawyer would
  add it: the clause closest in subject. A term that cannot be written (a value the term sheet
  leaves open) is a `[•]` with one open point, never left out.
- The base document's words stand wherever the term sheet and these rules do not reach. A first
  draft does not improve the house wording, however much better it would read.
- The term sheet's housekeeping is never written into the contract: that it is not binding, its
  own confidentiality, its expiry, its exclusivity period, the documentation timetable, approvals
  still to be obtained, and who pays for the drafting.
- A term defined in the base is used by its defined name. Where the term sheet calls the thing
  by another name, the base's name is written. A new defined term is added only where the
  contract uses it more than once, and it goes into the definitions in alphabetical order.
- A party is named in full, as the term sheet names it, the first time the contract names it,
  with its company number and registered office where the base asks for them; after that, by its
  defined name.
- Figures are written as the term sheet writes them, words and numerals both where the base
  writes both ("thirty (30) days"). A currency is written as the base writes currencies. Nothing
  is computed, converted or rounded.
- Dates the term sheet gives are written as dates; a date to be fixed at signing stays `[•]`.
- A bracketed option in the base is resolved: the alternative this deal takes is kept without
  its brackets, the others struck whole. A bracketed blank is filled from the term sheet or left
  `[•]` with one open point.
- A clause, schedule or definition that exists only for a kind of deal this is not is struck
  whole, with every reference to it. One that this deal does not need but the base gives as the
  house position stands.
- An open point says why before it asks: the term sheet's words, or the gap, then the question
  for the lawyer. One open point per question, on the paragraph it is about.
- Where the term sheet departs from a position the client's template states as settled
  (unbracketed words), the term sheet's term is written and one open point names the template
  position it departs from, so the lawyer can see the departure. A bracketed value or option is
  a blank the deal fills, never a house position: filling it from the term sheet raises no open
  point.
- When the base is a precedent, its own deal's terms (parties, purpose, figures, periods, governing
  law, concessions) are that deal's, never a house position: each is replaced by this deal's with
  no open point. A term the old deal added that this term sheet does not give (an extra category,
  a right, a carve-out) is struck, with one open point naming it, so the lawyer can keep it if the
  new deal wants it.
- Where the term sheet contradicts itself, neither version is chosen: the contract carries the
  first, and one open point quotes both.
- Governing law and jurisdiction are the term sheet's where it states them; otherwise the base's
  stand, with no open point.
- A cross-reference written in new text names the clause by its number and heading as the
  contract now numbers it. The base's own cross-references are never retyped.
